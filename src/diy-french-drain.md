A French drain looks simple: dig a trench, lay pipe, add gravel. Plenty of homeowners consider doing it themselves to save money. Here's an honest look at what the job really takes in a Raleigh yard, so you can decide with real numbers.

## Cost: the savings are smaller than they look

Materials are cheap on paper. Drain pipe runs about $0.50 to $3 per foot and gravel $25 to $53 per ton, which is why LawnStarter puts a typical DIY job near $700 against about $3,000 for a pro.<sup><a href="#s2">2</a></sup>

Then the extras show up: a trencher rental (typically $120 to $400 a day)<sup><a href="#s5">5</a></sup>, filter fabric, fittings, a catch basin, gravel delivery, and seed or sod to patch the lawn. When Lawn Love priced out a 25-foot DIY drain with tools and materials, it came to about $21 per foot, and they noted you might spend more doing it yourself than hiring out.<sup><a href="#s7">7</a></sup>

**Worked example, 60-foot drain in Raleigh.** At $20 to $50 per foot<sup><a href="#s3">3</a></sup>, a pro would charge roughly $1,200 to $3,000. A DIY version might save you roughly $500 to $2,300, in exchange for two or more days of hard labor and all the risk if the slope is off.

## Time and labor: Raleigh clay is the hard part

A yard French drain trench is typically at least 12 inches wide and 18 to 24 inches deep.<sup><a href="#s8">8</a></sup> For a 60-foot drain at 1 foot wide and 2 feet deep, that's about 4.4 cubic yards of soil to dig, move and haul away, and about the same volume of gravel to wheel back in.

In Raleigh that soil is often packed red clay: heavy when dry, sticky when wet. Forbes Advisor lists a basic French drain at 8 or more hours of working time and 10 or more hours total, at an intermediate skill level.<sup><a href="#s4">4</a></sup> For most homeowners that means a full weekend, sometimes two.

## Know-how: where DIY drains fail

{% figure "french-drain-cross-section.svg", "Cross-section of a French drain: fabric-lined gravel trench with a perforated pipe at the bottom", "Every layer matters: fabric, washed gravel, pipe placement and a steady downhill slope." %}

- **Slope.** The pipe needs to fall at least 1 inch for every 8 feet, steadily, all the way to the outlet.<sup><a href="#s9">9</a></sup> Over 60 feet that's about 7.5 inches of drop, and one flat or low section can stall the whole drain. Slope is where many DIY drains fail, and getting it right over a long run takes a laser level or transit.<sup><a href="#s8">8</a></sup>
- **Gravel and fabric.** Cheap gravel with fines, or skipping the filter fabric, lets silt clog the drain over time.<sup><a href="#s8">8</a></sup>
- **Pipe placement.** Perforated pipe goes in with the holes facing down.<sup><a href="#s8">8</a></sup>
- **The outlet.** The water has to go somewhere lower and legal. In clay, a poor outlet can just move the bog a few feet over.
- **Utilities.** North Carolina law requires calling NC 811 at least three full working days before you dig.<sup><a href="#s10">10</a></sup>

## When DIY makes sense

- The run is short, roughly 50 feet or less<sup><a href="#s1">1</a></sup>
- There's an obvious lower spot on your own property for the water to exit
- The drain is out in the yard, not along the foundation
- You have the time, the tools, and you're comfortable renting a trencher

## When to call a pro

- The drain is longer than about 50 feet, or the yard is mostly flat
- Water is reaching the foundation, crawl space or basement
- There's no obvious outlet and you may need a dry well or a line to the street
- The drain has to cross a driveway, walkway or tree roots
- You'd rather spend the weekend on something else

A local pro can usually tell you on the phone whether your yard is a simple job or not, and a free estimate shows you exactly what you'd save by doing it yourself.
